## FLAGS CARRIED FOR ROBERT

1. **The doubled “rules” renderings.** Throughout the book, the base's “statutes” and “ordinances” are both rendered “rules,” matching the lane's standing rendering in Leviticus. Where the base names both words in one breath, the draft therefore reads “rules” for both, word for word apart from that disclosed rendering. The places are Deuteronomy 4:1, 4:5, 4:8, 4:14, and 4:45 (“rules and rules” and its forms, in chapter 4:5 for example “I have taught you rules and rules”); Deuteronomy 5:1 and 5:31; Deuteronomy 6:1 and 6:20; Deuteronomy 7:11 (“the rules, and the rules”); Deuteronomy 8:11 (“his rules, and his rules”); Deuteronomy 11:1 (“his rules, his rules”) and 11:32 (“all the rules and the rules”); Deuteronomy 12:1 (“the rules and the rules”); Deuteronomy 26:16 (“these rules and rules”) and 26:17 (“his rules, his commandments, and his rules”); and Deuteronomy 30:16 (“his rules, and his rules”). In chapters where only one of the two base words occurs, the rendering is single and no doubling arises, as at Deuteronomy 10:13, 16:12, 17:19, 27:10, 28:15 and 28:45, and 33:10 and 33:21. Every doubling above is flagged in its chapter's apparatus, the auditors carried the flags rather than settling them, and they ride for Robert's read-through: whether a plainer distinction is wanted at those places is his call.

2. **Deuteronomy 16:16, the feast name — PENDING ROBERT.** At verses 3 and 8 of chapter 16, “unleavened bread” is a common description of food, and the draft's “bread without yeast” there is accepted by the auditor as ordinary plain-word work, on the Leviticus 7:12 and 8:2, 26 pattern, so those two verses stand. Verse 16 is different, and it is left undecided on purpose. In verse 16 the words sit in a list of three festival names, and the other two names in that list, “the feast of weeks” and “the feast of booths,” are both kept whole as names, as “Passover” is kept whole as a name everywhere in the chapter and the lane. Rendering only this one name descriptively, as “the feast of bread without yeast,” therefore breaks the parallelism of the name list and treats a name as a description. Against that, the name is a transparent one, Robert's plain-words principle favors wording a humble reader takes in at once, and “unleavened” is not a plain word. The base snapshot settles what the base says, “the feast of unleavened bread.” It does not settle whether name status protects the phrase, and that is a naming judgment of the kind Robert reserves to himself. The auditor therefore neither accepted the rendering as final nor rewrote the verse on audit authority. The verse stands exactly as drafted, and the question carried to Robert is the one his auditor stated: keep the base's feast name whole at verse 16, or keep the plain rendering for consistency with verses 3 and 8.

3. **The “soul” line, as the audits settled it.** The line the audits held, chapter by chapter, is this. Where “soul” stands for a countable person — a person who may be cut off, counted in a total, or made the subject of a ruling — the lane renders it “person” or “people,” as at Leviticus 19:8 and Leviticus 18:29, which the audits cite as the settled examples. Where “soul” names the inner self alongside the heart, or appetite and desire, or the self in address or comparison, or an inward grief, the base's word “soul” is kept, because “person” put in its place would break the phrase and change its sense. In Deuteronomy every occurrence fell on the keep side of that line: twenty occurrences in eighteen verses, each one accepted by its auditor, and the whole list is carried here so Robert can overrule any of it at his read-through. In the heart-and-soul pair, naming the inner self: Deuteronomy 4:9 (“keep your soul diligently”), 4:29, 6:5, 10:12, 11:13, 11:18 (“in your soul”), 13:3, 26:16, and 30:2, 30:6, and 30:10, each in the fixed words “with all your heart and with all your soul,” apart from 4:9 and 11:18 as quoted. Naming appetite and the desiring self: Deuteronomy 12:15, 12:20 (twice, “because your soul desires to eat meat” and “after all the desire of your soul”), and 12:21; Deuteronomy 14:26 (twice, “whatever your soul desires” and “whatever your soul asks of you”); and Deuteronomy 18:6 (“with all the desire of his soul,” of the Levite, who is named in that verse by “a Levite,” not by “soul”). In the comparison idiom: Deuteronomy 13:6, the friend “who is as your own soul,” where one's own soul is the measure of closeness and the person is named by “friend.” Naming inward grief: Deuteronomy 28:65, “pining of soul,” standing with “a trembling heart” and “failing of eyes” in the description of the state Yahweh will give the scattered people. No countable-person occurrence of “soul” arises in this book, so no “soul” in Deuteronomy is rendered “person” or “people.” The audits state the distinction itself, and not a blanket keeping or a blanket changing of the word, as the lane-consistent line, and Deuteronomy 30's auditor carried its three verses as still pending Robert, because the final word on a flagged verse is his.

4. **The descriptive-“will” line, and the judgment calls the auditors sustained with flags.** Audit Crew M-1 stated the line in full at chapter 28, so that one line could be held across blocks, and this report carries its statement whole. “Must” renders “shall” where the base lays a command, a prohibition, or a prescribed action on the person addressed. “Will” renders it where the base states what will be, whether as promise, outcome, or condition. In M-1's own summary: a descriptive or conditional “shall” — one that states what will be the case, in a census, a blessing, a curse, or a condition — takes “will”; “must” is reserved for “shall” that itself does the commanding. Chapter 28 is the line's largest application: all fourteen of its “shall” forms sit inside the blessings and curses as states, outcomes, and conditions, so all fourteen are rendered “will” and none “must,” and the auditor accepted that chapter-wide, with the three condition forms as the nearest calls — verse 1, second occurrence (“if you will listen diligently”), verse 9 (“if you will keep the commandments”), and verse 14 (“and will not turn away,” continuing the condition of verses 13–14). The same line is applied at Deuteronomy 29:19, where the chapter's single “shall” is a man's quoted self-blessing, “I will have peace, though I walk in the stubbornness of my heart,” accepted as a prediction he makes for himself, not a command to anyone. On that same line, the auditors sustained the following judgment calls, each as disclosed in its chapter's apparatus and each carried here for Robert's read-through, none of them blocking. At Deuteronomy 3:19, “will live” is accepted, because the wives, little ones, and livestock are third persons in a stated future arrangement, not persons receiving a command. At Deuteronomy 4:29–30, “will seek” and “will return” are accepted, because both stand in Moses' prophecy of what will happen in exile, and verse 29's own next clause in the base already reads “you will find him.” At Deuteronomy 7:24, “will” is accepted as an outcome in the promise sequence of verses 22–24, and its arguable prescribed-action reading stays flagged; the conditional “will” at 7:17 is accepted on the same condition ground. At Deuteronomy 8:19 the “will” conversions are accepted, the first inside the condition “if you will forget” and the third stating the outcome “you will surely perish,” and at 8:9–10 the eating is the land's promised outcome and takes “will,” while the blessing at verse 10 is the prescribed act and takes “must.” At Deuteronomy 9:3, “So you will drive them out” is accepted as the outcome of Yahweh's going over before them as a devouring fire, with its arguable “must” reading flagged. At Deuteronomy 10:11, “they will go in and possess the land” is accepted as the promised outcome of Yahweh's oath inside his quoted speech, not a command addressed to the people. At Deuteronomy 11:13 and 11:22 the conditional “will” forms are accepted, and at 11:31 “you will possess it and dwell in it” is accepted as a promised outcome. At Deuteronomy 12:5, 12:11, 12:18, 12:21, and 12:26, “the place which Yahweh your God will choose” is accepted, because it states Yahweh's future choosing, not a command to the reader. At Deuteronomy 12:7 the “must” reading is accepted and stays flagged as arguable, because eating and rejoicing before Yahweh at the chosen place is a prescribed action, and at Deuteronomy 12:22 the “must” reading is likewise accepted and stays flagged as arguable, because eating the meat within the gates stands in the instruction sequence of verses 21–22. At Deuteronomy 13:11 and 13:17 the “will” choices are accepted, the first as a purpose-and-result statement about the people and the second because the devoted thing clinging to the hand is a required state stated of the thing, with no addressed agent to bear a “must”; the same purpose-and-result formula is ruled the same way at Deuteronomy 17:13. At Deuteronomy 15:10, “your heart must not be grieved” keeps “must,” because the clause stands in a pair with the command “You must surely give” and prohibits an inner attitude of the addressed “you,” while at 15:22 the second conversion keeps “will,” because “The unclean and the clean will eat it alike” states who may eat, an eligibility, and reading it as “must” would command the unclean person to eat, which the base does not say. At Deuteronomy 16:4 the pair keeps “will,” because no yeast being seen and no meat remaining all night are required states stated of the yeast and the meat, with no addressed agent, and at 16:21 “which you will make for yourselves” keeps “will,” because the clause describes the altar's making and commands nothing. At Deuteronomy 17:9, “they must give you the verdict” keeps “must,” because giving the verdict is the priests' and the judge's prescribed part in the inquiry procedure of verses 8–11. At Deuteronomy 18:1–2 and 18:8 the Levites' lot keeps “will,” because those verses state the tribe's standing portion as a settled state rather than commanding any action of them, while at 18:7 “then he must minister” keeps “must” for the coming Levite's prescribed service act, and at 18:10 “There will not be found with you anyone who…” keeps “will,” because the prohibition is stated impersonally as a state of the community. At Deuteronomy 19:4–5 the manslayer's fleeing is rendered with “will,” sustained as the provision the law makes for him rather than a command laid on him, and at 19:15 “will a matter be established” keeps the base's own word order as the outcome of the two-or-three-witness rule. At Deuteronomy 20:19, “When you will besiege a city a long time” is sustained, read as the future case the law addresses rather than a command to besiege. At Deuteronomy 21:9, “when you will do that which is right” is sustained on the same future-case ground, and at Deuteronomy 21:13 the captive woman's taking off her clothing, remaining in the house, and the man's going in to her after the month are rendered with “must” as the prescribed procedure the law lays down, with “she will be your wife” in the same verse rendered “will” as the outcome. At Deuteronomy 22:22 and 22:25 the death outcomes rendered with “will” (“they will both die”; “only the man who lay with her will die”) were verified against the lane's settled practice at Leviticus 19:20 and Numbers 18:7 and sustained, the community's own stoning duty at 22:21 and 22:24 being separately rendered with “must.” At Deuteronomy 23:16–17 the escaped servant's dwelling and choosing, and the community state of verse 17, are rendered with “will,” sustained as the arrangement and state the law secures rather than commands laid on the servant. At Deuteronomy 30:8, “You will return and obey” is accepted as rendered and flagged PENDING ROBERT, because verses 1–7 describe the future restoration as something that will happen and verse 8 continues that described sequence, while the exhortation reading, which would take “must,” is disclosed in the apparatus. Last in this flag, the “must not go” line stands on the prohibition side of the same rule at three places, each accepted by its auditor, each with the outcome reading (“will not go”) disclosed in its apparatus, and each carried PENDING ROBERT: Deuteronomy 31:2, “You must not go over this Jordan,” Yahweh's own quoted prohibition spoken to Moses; Deuteronomy 32:52, “but you must not go there into the land,” where the same verse splits the two uses, the seeing rendered “will” and the barring “must not”; and Deuteronomy 34:4, “but you must not go over there,” where Yahweh speaks directly to Moses over the land he has just shown him. In each place the auditors ruled that the words bar Moses from crossing; they do not merely predict that he will not cross.
