## Item 1 — The deliberative "shall"

**The question Robert is asked to rule.** In a number of places the base text puts a question into a speaker's mouth in which the speaker is weighing an act of their own — what they ought to do, or say, or ask — and the base words that question with "shall": "What shall I do?", "Shall we go up?", "What shall I say?". The campaign has not treated those questions uniformly. Some stand converted to "will," at least one stands converted to "must," one stands converted to "should," and many stand with "shall" kept exactly as the base gives it. Robert is asked to rule which treatment governs this kind of question everywhere: keep the base's "shall," render "will," or render "should." One ruling settles every place below, because each place is named, and a later fix pass can apply the ruling mechanically to all of them.

**The three positions on record.** Three audit positions stand on the record, and none has been adopted. The first position is to keep "shall," and it rests on a precedent finding: Audit Crew U-1, auditing Ruth, reported that the audited Genesis and Exodus practice actually keeps deliberative "shall" — Genesis 30:31 asks "What shall I give you?" with "shall" standing, Exodus 2:7 and Exodus 17:4 likewise retain it, and the "will I" questions in Genesis (at 15:8; 18:12, 13, 17; 37:10; and 38:18) are the base's own wording, kept rather than converted — so on that finding, conversion is the departure from precedent, not the keeping (Ruth completion report, Flags item 1; checkpoint, 2026-10-16). The second position is "will": Audit Crew O-1, at Joshua 7:8, reasoned that a deliberative question is not a command, that "must" would impose a false obligation, and that "will" is the least-wrong plain equivalent (Joshua completion report, Flags item 1). The third position is "should": Audit Crew S-1, at Judges 13:12, proposed "should" for questions that weigh what ought to be done, reserving "will" for pure questions of future fact and "must" for statements of requirement, never for questions (Judges completion report, Flags item 1).

**The classification ruling that governs what belongs on this item.** The 2 Peter audit ruled that the item's test is functional, not modal: a question belongs on this item when it functions as deliberation about a course of action, whatever word carries it, so an "ought" question belongs here the same way a "shall" question does (2 Peter completion report, Flags item 1). The same audit fixed the boundary on the other side, and 1 John's report states the distinction in its sharpest form: candidacy was rightly refused at 1 John 5:5 — "Who is he who overcomes the world, but he who believes…?" — because what is settled there is an identity, not a course of action; whereas James 3:13 — "Who is wise and understanding among you?" — does meet the candidate ground, because although it is identity-shaped, it functions as a summons, and its own verse prescribes the course by imperative: "Let him show by his good conduct…" (1 John completion report, Flags item 3; James completion report, Flags item 1).

## Item 3 — The soul family

**The three-branch line the crews worked by.** Where the base's word "soul" counts a person — where it is the counting word in a tally of people — the lane renders "person" or "people" (branch 1). Where "soul" names the inner self — standing alongside the heart in the heart-and-soul pair, or naming appetite and desire, or addressed by the speaker as his own self, or bowed in grief — the lane keeps "soul" (branch 2). Where "soul" is the self bound or afflicted in the reflexive idiom — "bind his soul" in a vow, "afflict your souls" in the fast — the lane keeps "soul" (branch 3), grounded in Leviticus 16:29 and 31, "afflict your souls," kept and passed, and Numbers 29:7. The line was formed by the coordinator on 2026-10-16 as the audit crews converged on it place by place (checkpoint, 2026-10-16), and Audit Crew G-1's Numbers 30 ruling completed it in its three branches. One rider rides with branch 3: if Robert prefers the reflexive rendering ("bound herself," "bound himself"), it must be applied to Leviticus 16, Numbers 29:7, and Numbers 30 together, not to one alone (Numbers report, Flags item 4).

**The reserved family — the keeps this item exists to decide.** Across the branches stands a fourth company, and it is the heart of this item. Where an outcome — salvation, destruction, death, deliverance — is predicated of the soul itself, the crews kept "soul" and flagged the place for Robert's ruling rather than deciding it by the branch test, because converting there would silently settle what kind of thing a soul is and what can happen to it. **The headline of this item — 1 Peter 3:20, the boundary case, with both positions in full.** The verse stands as drafted: "In it, few, that is, eight people, were saved through water," where the base reads "eight souls." The reader carries "eight people"; no reader edit was made on account of the dissent recorded below. (Source for the whole entry: 1 Peter completion report, Flags item 2.)

The drafter's position (Draft Crew PE1-A, chapter 3 apparatus) is that branch 1 governs: "soul" counting a person renders "person" or "people." On the drafter's reading, branch 1 fits in its purest form. The verse counts persons expressly, with a numeral — "eight" — with an appositive tally gloss, "few, that is," and with a located company, those in the ship while it was being built; "souls" is the counting word of the verse. Branch 2 does not fit, because no inner life, heart, appetite, address, or grief is named — the eight are tallied, not described inwardly — and branch 3 does not apply. The counting precedents the drafter cited were verified real in their audited Acts files: Acts 2:41, "about three thousand souls" rendered "people"; Acts 7:14, "seventy-five souls" rendered "people"; Acts 27:37, "two hundred seventy-six people on the ship" — with the Old Testament counts at Genesis 46 and Exodus 1:5 besides. And the reserved question was expressly tested by the drafter, not passed over. In form, "were saved" is predicated of the counted eight, and the reserved family keeps "soul" where a salvation outcome is predicated of the soul itself standing as referent. The drafter's distinction is that at 3:20 the saved are the counted persons in the ship, rescued through the water as persons — the "souls" do not stand alone as the referent of a salvation predicated of the soul as such — so the counting construction governs the word's function in the verse. The instance was flagged into the boundary record either way, as a record only, settling nothing.

The auditor's dissent (Audit Crew PE1-AUD, recorded in full in chapter 3's audit record) is a dissent on the reserved side of a genuine boundary case. The auditor verified the base wording and the counting ground in full: all three Acts counting places stand audited as branch-1 conversions, and the ship-company parallel at Acts 27:37 is real and close. The auditor then recorded the distinguishing fact. In none of the three Acts counting places is an outcome predicated of the souls: Acts 2:41 tallies persons added; Acts 7:14 tallies relatives summoned; Acts 27:37 totals a ship's company, its auditor expressly noting that the word "predicates nothing of a soul itself." Whereas every audited reserved keep carries an outcome predicated of the soul itself: Acts 3:23, destruction, kept — its auditor ruling that the branch test is not grammar alone, that predication is the reserved trigger, and that a tally-shaped distribution "distributes the sanction; it does not remove it"; Hebrews 10:39, "faith to the saving of the soul," kept; and James 1:21, "able to save your souls," kept, with the James 5:20 pattern besides. At 3:20, both triggers stand in one clause: the souls are counted, and a salvation outcome is predicated of them — "eight souls, were saved through water" — and this book's own 1:9 keeps "souls" where salvation is predicated of them. On the Acts 3:23 logic, a tally does not by itself remove a predicated outcome, so the counting precedents do not cleanly govern a verse that also carries the saved predicate. The auditor therefore ruled that the reserved pattern governs the classification. The auditor stated the drafter's distinction fairly in its own record — it is a real reading — and dissented from its governing force, not from its honesty.

The disposition, stated exactly as the report carries it: the conversion stands as drafted, unrevised; no fix was imposed; the instance is classified a genuine boundary case whose provisional side is the drafted "people"; and the question — counting ground or predication trigger — is pending Robert as an item-3 boundary record.

## The "iniquity" split practice

The base's word "iniquity" has not been treated one way across the campaign.
