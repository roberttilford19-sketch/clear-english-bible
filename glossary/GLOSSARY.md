# A Reader's Glossary to the Clear Translation Bible

This glossary explains the words and word-choices a reader will meet in the Clear Translation Bible — the working title of the Clear English Bible, the clear-English modernization of the public-domain World English Bible Classic base prepared under the editorship of Robert Tilford. It is compiled entirely from the apparatus of the campaign's seventy-three book completion reports, the files in the `reports/` folder of this shelf, and every entry names the report or reports it rests on. Nothing in it is drawn from outside the reports. Where the reports record a disagreement, a split practice, or a question left open for the editor's ruling, the glossary records it as it stands; a glossary of this work does not settle what the reports left open.

Most entries describe a *practice* rather than a definition: how a word was carried, converted, or kept, and why. Decisions the reports mark PENDING ROBERT are the editor's to make at his read-through, and several entries say so where they stand.

---

## The divine name: "Yahweh"

The divine name is carried exactly as the base gives it, per occurrence, and the governing rule is stated the same way across the reports: no form is harmonized toward another, and none is supplied where the base does not give it. Where the Hebrew base carries the Tetragrammaton, the text reads "Yahweh," following Robert's 2026-10-12 ruling (Parameter 1); where a fetched base page itself printed "LORD," the draft renders "Yahweh" under that ruling and discloses the fact in the book's apparatus. The name appears throughout the Old Testament in the base's own distribution — Psalms carries 697 occurrences (691 in the verses and 6 in the superscriptions), Numbers 397, Ezekiel 435, Zechariah 132, Proverbs 87, Zephaniah 34, Ruth 18, Nehemiah 17, Nahum 13, Obadiah 7 — and it is absent wherever the base is absent of it. Nineteen psalms carry no "Yahweh" at all, eight chapters of Nehemiah carry none, and whole books carry none: the Song of Songs has none in this base, and nothing was supplied into it. In the New Testament the base carries no Tetragrammaton, so "Yahweh" occurs zero times in every New Testament book, in base and draft alike; the reports state the principle in one sentence that recurs book after book: its absence is the base's state, not a defect, and no "Yahweh" has been imported from the Old Testament practice. The Greek-source books stand the same way — Sirach, Wisdom, Tobit, Esther, and Ecclesiastes read "Lord," "God," and "Most High" as their bases give them, and nothing was harmonized in.

## The name's compounds and short forms

The compounds stand unharmonized, each as its own base gives it. "Lord Yahweh" is carried exactly — 217 of Ezekiel's 435 occurrences, 20 times in Amos, once each in Zephaniah (1:7), Zechariah (9:14), and Obadiah (1:1) — and the "Lord" inside the compound is never counted again as a standalone. Standalone "the Lord," where the base's word is a different word from the Name, is likewise carried exactly and never replaced: Tobit's base reads "the Lord" throughout and no "Yahweh" was substituted anywhere in that book; James reads "the Lord" some fifty times, carried as it stands, and at James 5:4 the base's own compound reads "the Lord of Armies," carried exactly. The fuller compound stands as "Yahweh of Armies" — that is this base's word, "Armies," and no report in the corpus uses or converts a "hosts" form; it occurs 53 times in Zechariah, 14 in Haggai, and twice each in Nahum and Zephaniah. The short form "Yah" is carried exactly and counted separately, never expanded to "Yahweh": 41 times in the Psalms, once in the Song of Songs (8:6, "a very flame of Yah"), and at Exodus 15:2 and 17:16 in the Classic base's own short form. Capital forms the base itself prints are kept in capitals: "YAHWEH your God" at Deuteronomy 28:58, and "HOLY TO YAHWEH" at Exodus 28:36 and 39:30.

## Other names and titles of God

Other divine titles are carried as each base gives them, counted as their own forms and never levelled into one another. "Most High" is kept wherever it stands — 44 times in Sirach, twice in Wisdom — and where Sirach's base reads "the Highest" instead (28:7), "the Highest" is what stands, flagged and not levelled. Wisdom's fuller address "Sovereign Lord" occurs five times and is counted inside its "Lord" total. Baruch carries "Lord Almighty" twice, "the Everlasting" as a title (with lowercase "everlasting" for covenant, God, joy, and hills carried exactly and not counted in the title), and "the Holy One" three times. Where a title does not occur, the reports say so rather than pass over it: Ezekiel, for instance, carries no "Most High" anywhere, in base or draft.

*Sources: SIRACH-, WISDOM-, BARUCH-, HOSEA-, EZEKIEL-COMPLETION-REPORT.md.*

## "Soul" — the countable-person branch

The word "soul" in the base does several different jobs, and the campaign treated it by the job it is doing, in three branches the reports state in one shared sentence: where "soul" stands for a countable person — a person who can be listed, tallied, or cut off — the text renders "person" or "people"; where "soul" names the inner self or appetite, "soul" is kept as the base's word; where "soul" stands in the reflexive idiom of binding or afflicting oneself, "soul" is likewise kept. Under the first branch, Acts reads "people" at 2:41 ("about three thousand people"), "every person" at 2:43, and "people" at 7:14 and 27:37; Genesis 46 reads "people" through its census verses with every total verified unchanged; Numbers converts the cut-off cases (5:6; 9:13; 15:28, 30, 31; 19:13, 20, 22; 31:28); Romans 13:1 reads "Let every person be in subjection to the higher authorities," the reports noting that no outcome is predicated of the soul there. Proverbs 11:25 reads "The liberal person will be made fat" and 11:30 "wins people," both ruled person-references classified by character rather than souls under an outcome. Sirach converts three places (14:8; 26:14; 26:15) and Wisdom one (10:7, "a disbelieving person"). One status difference stands on the record: Deuteronomy and Acts treat these dispositions as standing on the settled line, while Genesis leaves its census renderings as the book's one open flag for the editor's ruling at read-through.

*Sources: NUMBERS-, TOBIT-, DEUTERONOMY-, ACTS-, GENESIS-COMPLETION-REPORT-2026-10-13.md, ROMANS-, PROVERBS-, SIRACH-, WISDOM-, PHILIPPIANS-COMPLETION-REPORT.md.*

## "Soul" — the inner-self branch

Under the second branch, "soul" is kept because "person" put in its place would break the phrase and change its sense. Deuteronomy keeps all twenty of its occurrences, in four named registers: the heart-and-soul pair ("with all your heart and with all your soul"); appetite and the desiring self; the comparison idiom (the friend "who is as your own soul"); and inward grief. Tobit's five occurrences are all of this branch, from "I remembered God with all my soul" to "Let my soul bless God the great King." The Song of Songs keeps its five, all in the desire register ("my soul loves"). Acts keeps four, including "of one heart and soul" at 4:32; Ecclesiastes keeps five; Baruch keeps four; Hebrews keeps, among others, "an anchor of the soul" (6:19) and "dividing of soul and spirit" (4:12), where the word is one term of a divided pair. Revelation 18:14 is kept on the appetite ground: "The fruits which your soul lusted after" — the outcomes in that verse are predicated of the fruits, not of the soul. The Psalter works this branch through a set of settled sub-classes the audits built book by book — first-person pleas where the soul stands as object of a save or deliver verb beside a parallel clause naming the speaker himself; purpose infinitives with no survival outcome; deliverance already accomplished and told in retrospect; the enemies'-intent idiom; and collective inner states — and records no countable-person conversion anywhere in the Psalms' 130 occurrences. Through all of it, "soul" and "spirit" are never interconverted: Wisdom states the rule outright, and at Ecclesiastes 6:7 the base's own word is "appetite," which the draft carries as "appetite," exactly.

## "Soul" — the reflexive branch, and a numbering difference the reports leave open

The third branch, as Numbers and Tobit state it, is the reflexive idiom: "you must afflict your souls" (Numbers 29:7, on the Leviticus 16 precedent) and the twelve vow-binding occurrences of "bind his soul" in Numbers 30, kept because the idiom is load-bearing legal language and neither "bound her person" nor "bound herself" carries it without rewriting every clause; Numbers' audit adds the rider that if the editor prefers a reflexive rendering, it must be applied to Leviticus 16, Numbers 29:7, and Numbers 30 together, so the line stays one line. The reports do not number this branch uniformly, and the glossary records the difference rather than smoothing it: Galatians names the three branches as countable-person conversion, inner-self keeping, and reflexive keeping, while Habakkuk places its reflexive case (2:10, the self a man wrongs by his own act) under branch 2, and Proverbs likewise describes 6:32 ("He who does it destroys his own soul") as a reflexive-self reference settled under its branch-2 wording. Acts, meanwhile, counts its keeps as branch-2 keeps plus a separate, unnumbered family — the Reserved family of the next entry — so the label "branch 3" means the reflexive keeping in one report and sits beside the reserved keeps in another. The practice is consistent; the numbering is not, and no crew settled it.

## "Soul" — the Reserved family (PENDING ROBERT)

One family of "soul" places was deliberately left undecided, and it is the largest open item in this glossary. Where an outcome — life, death, survival, deliverance, destruction, anguish — is predicated of the soul itself, the word "soul" is kept exactly as drafted and flagged for the editor's ruling, because converting it would silently decide the very question he reserved, and keeping it is the only disposition that decides nothing. Quotation or application makes no difference to the test. Acts holds three such keeps (2:27; 2:31; and 3:23, "every soul that will not listen to that prophet will be utterly destroyed from among the people," whose grammar is countable-person in shape but whose outcome is life-or-death and legal-communal). Romans 2:9 is kept on the same ground — wrath and anguish predicated of "every soul of man who does evil" — with distribution expressly ruled not to remove it. The Psalter contributes twelve members, all kept exactly; Ezekiel six audit-ruled members and thirteen candidates; Hebrews 10:39 ("to the saving of the soul") is kept on the Acts 3:23 pattern; and the membership counts grew as the audits proceeded — sixteen at the Psalms report, twenty at Wisdom's, twenty-four at Sirach's fifty-first chapter, then twenty-nine, thirty-two, thirty-five, and thirty-nine through Isaiah, Jeremiah, Hebrews, and 1 Peter, standing at fifty-eight after Revelation. One ruling on this family, when the editor gives it, will apply back uniformly across all its places in every book.

The family's hardest place is 1 Peter 3:20, and the reports carry both positions in full. The drafter converted there — "in which a few, that is, eight people, were saved through water" — on counting precedents: Jonah 1:3, where "souls" stands under a tally of persons in the ship's company, and 2 Peter 2:5, where Noah is "the eighth person" in a counted group of eight. The auditor dissented in full: at every counting precedent, the counting attaches to something other than the counted persons themselves, while at 1 Peter 3:20 all eight counted units are the souls themselves, with no counted thing distinct from them; the auditor would have kept "eight souls." The editor's ruling settled it: on 2026-10-18 Robert ruled that, an outcome ("were saved through water") being predicated of the souls at this verse, the base's word stands — the text reads "few, that is, eight souls, were saved through water," as it stands on this shelf — and the drafter's conversion does not stand. The ruling adopted the auditor's position; the drafter's counting ground stands recorded above as the losing reading, preserved, not erased. (This entry's earlier wording, which carried the conversion as standing provisionally, was corrected to the shelf as ratified at the Gate B pass, 2026-10-25, under disposition DP5.)

The family also has settled edges — places tested and ruled out, which are as much a part of the practice as the members. An accomplished-retrospective deliverance does not engage it (the Psalms class above); a state predicated of the soul is not an outcome predicated of it, a refinement the audits reached at 2 Peter 2:8 and 3 John 2, where the predicates are vexation and health; a prize or an examination is not a survival outcome (Wisdom 2:22; 3:13); different-referent places stand outside (Sheol at Amos 9:2 is a different word from "soul" and engages nothing); and praise is not an outcome (Greek Daniel 3:86). Two disagreements inside the family stand unresolved on the record: at Psalms, one crew ruled Psalm 22:20 a settled keep on its "my precious life" parallel while a later crew reserved Psalm 35:17 and cited 22:20 as a reserved-boundary parallel — both records stand as written; and at Revelation 6:9 and 20:4, both kept as a pair that the auditors ruled stands or falls together, a reserved counter-reading is recorded at full strength for 20:4 alone, where life *is* predicated of the souls as subject — "they lived and reigned with Christ." One further one-off stands disclosed: at Numbers 31:50 the draft reads "to make atonement for ourselves," which the auditor neither fixed nor approved as settled; the three options framed for the editor are to keep "ourselves" as a one-off, to render "our persons," or to keep the base's "our souls."

## "Shall," "must," and "will"

The base's "shall" was rendered by one governing test, stated in Greek Daniel and held across the lane: whether the "shall" itself commands. Where the base lays a command, a prohibition, or a prescribed action on the person addressed, the text reads "must"; where the base states what will be — promise, outcome, condition, census, blessing, or curse — the text reads "will." Deuteronomy's audit stated the line in full so one line could be held across blocks, and the books' settlements follow it: Numbers' 613 base "shall"s become 322 "must" and 291 "will"; Psalms' 189 become 187 "will" and only 2 "must," both at Psalm 81:9 inside God's own forward prohibition; Proverbs' 63 all become "will"; Amos's 19, Esther's 12, and Ecclesiastes' 30 all become "will." Quoted commandments take "must" as commanded content — "You must love your neighbor as yourself" at Galatians 5:14 and Romans 13:9 — and Hebrews 12:20 renders the commanded penalty inside the Exodus command "it must be stoned." A stated penalty outcome, by contrast, takes "will": the stranger who comes near "will be put to death" is a decreed outcome, while the community's duty to carry out an execution is a prescribed act and stands in "must." Occurrences of "will," "must," and "should" that are the base's own wording are never counted as conversions and are carried unchanged. One dissent stands on the record unapplied: at Esther 5:1 the royal "You shall not die" stands rendered "You will not die" on the assurance/outcome ruling, against an auditor's dissent that a royal word of that shape can be heard as a protective bar, nearer the prohibition family.

## The deliberative "shall" (PENDING ROBERT)

A "shall" inside a question, where the asker weighs his own genuinely open act, is kept exactly as the base gives it and gathered under one consolidated item for the editor's ruling, because converting it either way would decide the question he reserved. The audits grade the places: a MEMBER is a question whose hearers weigh their own act with the course genuinely open and a ruling sought from another — "Brothers, what shall we do?" (Acts 2:37) and "What shall I do, Lord?" (Acts 22:10); a CANDIDATE is deliberative in form but fails a member condition, as the council's "What shall we do to these men?" (Acts 4:16), where no ruling is sought and the direction is already settled; places ruled OUTSIDE the item are fact, possibility, rhetorical, rebuke, capacity, and settled-course questions; and deliberative-shaped questions that carry no "shall" in this base at all are recorded as DATA and change nothing. Romans keeps seven "shall"s, all candidates, because at every place the answer is built in or supplied by the speaker himself. Psalm 116:12 is the Psalter's only audit-ruled member, and it carries the base's own "will," so no ruling changes it. Two renderings at the item's edge stand flagged rather than settled. At Ruth 3:1 the draft rendered Naomi's words "will I not seek rest for you," and the auditor found that the drafter's claimed Genesis precedent does not exist — Genesis's "will I" questions are the base's own wording, kept and not converted — so the verse stands exactly as drafted, neither accepted as lane-consistent nor changed under audit authority, with the same question open at Joshua 7:8 and Judges 13:12; Tobit 5:14 ("what wages shall I give you?" rendered "what wages will I give you?") rides the same item with its placement concurred. And at Song of Songs 8:8, a place the auditor ruled a member of the item, the draft reads "What must we do for our sister" — a fourth rendering outside all three audit positions on the item (keep "shall," "will," "should") — standing as drafted until the editor's ruling on the consolidated item settles it.

## The arrangement and standing-duty "shall" (PENDING ROBERT)

A second gathered family holds the "shall"s of standing duty, office, roster, and prescribed rite, distinct from one-time commands. Its central question is framed in Numbers: is a prescribed arrangement in Yahweh's spoken order a description, which takes "will," or a prescription, which takes "must," as the lane's Leviticus practice for prescribed arrangements suggests? The books answered it in both directions on the same item, and both directions stand. Numbers rendered the camp arrangement of chapters 2–3 and the duty statements of chapters 4–5 mostly in "will" — twenty-six or more renderings standing flagged, neither overturned nor blessed as settled — while its auditor accepted "must" at 8:25–26 for the age-fifty Levite service rules. Ezekiel's temple-code chapters entered 110 standing-duty instances by chapter-and-verse reference, bookkeeping only, renderings standing. Ezra 6:8's "must" was placed expressly on this item rather than settled verse by verse, with the descriptive "will" counterweight disclosed; Nehemiah's three covenant-clause "must"s (10:38 twice; 10:39) join the same item; and Zechariah entered its standing-office places (3:7; 8:16–17) as bookkeeping only. One-time acts are excluded from the family by a named line: Amos 7:3 and 7:6 are one-time relenting decrees, Greek Daniel 3:10 a single decree's prescribed act, Hosea 3:3 a one-time sign-act instruction. The editor's single ruling on this item settles the uniform direction for all of it, and the change, once ruled, is mechanical.

*Sources: NUMBERS-, NEHEMIAH-, ZECHARIAH-, EZEKIEL-, EZRA-, AMOS-, DANIEL-GREEK-, HABAKKUK-, HAGGAI-, HOSEA-COMPLETION-REPORT.md.*

## "Behold" becomes "See"

The interjection "behold" is rendered "See" at a sentence head and "see" mid-sentence, the case following the position — the campaign's settled plain equivalent. The conversion runs through every book: Isaiah 97 places, Jeremiah 141, Ezekiel 110, Matthew 66, Luke 50, Zechariah 25, Acts 24, Revelation 23, John 18, Amos 15, Mark 12, and smaller counts everywhere else. Two qualifications ride with it, and one exception that stood beside them is retired below, on the record. Where "behold" is an infinitive verb rather than the interjection, it is kept: Susanna stands "beautiful to behold" (Greek Daniel 13:31). Where the base's own footnotes speak of the word "Behold" — a lexical note that stands at many places, "'Behold', from the Hebrew, means look at, take notice, observe, see, or gaze at" — the apparatus quotes it exactly and unconverted, so a reader will meet the old word in the notes of the very verses where the text now reads "See." And two kept instances long stood disclosed in the text itself: Numbers 23:24, "Behold, a people rises up as a lioness," and its precedent at Exodus 39:43, unconverted in base and draft alike. That disclosed-exception note is retired: Ruling 14 of the completion docket (2026-10-24) converted both places to "See" — Numbers 23:24 now reads "See, a people rises up as a lioness" — making the conversion exceptionless across the corpus, and the conversion stands recorded here in the retired note's place. Where the conversion doubles a "see" in one breath — Tobit 11:15 now reads "See, I see my son Tobias" — the doubling stands flagged for the read-through, not smoothed.

## "Iniquity" becomes "sin" — with a recorded split

The usual line renders "iniquity" as "sin" and "iniquities" as "sins," the plain equivalent throughout the lane: Ezekiel converts all 54 of its occurrences, Ezra its three, Hosea nine places, Habakkuk two, Sirach eight, and the New Testament at Acts 8:23, Romans 4:7, Titus 2:14, and Hebrews 1:9, where the word stands inside the Psalm 45 quotation. The practice is not uniform, and the reports carry the split openly for the editor's uniform ruling. Where "sin" already stands beside the word in the same sentence, "iniquity" is kept: at Exodus 20:5 (on the recorded ground that no plainer wording holds the sense without softening), at Exodus 34:7 twice and 34:9, at Hebrews 10:17 ("their sins and their iniquities I will remember no more"), at Leviticus 16:21 and 16:22, and at Revelation 18:5, where "her sins" stands immediately before "her iniquities" in the same sentence — so that book carries both sides of the split in adjacent chapters. The audits verified at Hebrews 1:9 that the keep-grounds do *not* fit there, which is why that place converts. Where the conversion produces a repetition — Romans 4:7's "whose sins are forgiven, whose sins are covered," Nehemiah 9:2's doubled "sins," Hosea 12:8's "any sin that is sin," where the base itself pairs "iniquity" with its own "sin" — the repetition stands disclosed in the apparatus, not smoothed away.

## The abomination family becomes "detestable"

"Abomination" is rendered "detestable thing," "abominations" as "detestable things," and "abominable" as "detestable," with the article adjusting as the rendering requires — "an abomination" becomes "a detestable thing." Ezekiel converts all 50 of its occurrences; Sirach ten; Greek Daniel its three (9:27; 11:31; 12:11); Ezra its three; Esther and Hosea the adjective at one place each, with no article supplied or removed at Hosea 9:10 because the base has none there. Where the converted phrase requires an article the base lacked, the article is supplied and disclosed case by case (Sirach 10:13). Revelation converts at four places, including 17:5, where the word stands inside the name inscribed on the woman's forehead and the capitals are kept — "DETESTABLE THINGS" — on the ground that the converted word is a common noun standing among the inscription's words, and converting it at 17:4 while keeping it at 17:5 would split one word in one breath. The counter-reading is recorded at full strength beside it: that a name's words can be argued to ride with the name as a whole, unconverted as "MYSTERY" and "BABYLON THE GREAT" ride. The editor rules; the glossary records both.

## Law words: "statutes" and "ordinances" become "rules"

Where Yahweh is the author of the law-word, "statutes" and "ordinances" are rendered "rules," singular matched with singular and plural with plural, on the lane's settled line: Romans at four places (1:32; 2:26; 8:4; 13:2), Colossians at 2:14 and 2:20, Ephesians 2:15 ("the law of commandments contained in rules"), Hebrews 9:1 and 9:10, Baruch twice, Amos 2:4, Greek Daniel 9:5, Ezra 7:10 and 7:11, Zephaniah 2:3, and throughout Deuteronomy and Ezekiel. The boundary is authorship, not quality: Ezekiel converts even at 20:25, "the rules that were not good," because Yahweh is their author. Human and royal law-words are kept and flagged instead — Greek Daniel's royal "statute" at 6:7 and 6:15, Esther's royal "ordinance" at 2:8, 8:14, and 8:17 (where "the king's rule" would misread as his reign), Ezekiel's "ordinances of the nations" and "statutes of your fathers" — all under a scope flag for the editor: if he extends the conversion to royal proclamations, those places change together. Where the base names both words in one breath, both render "rules" and the doubling stands flagged, never smoothed: "I have taught you rules and rules" at Deuteronomy 4:1 and some sixteen further Deuteronomy places, Nehemiah 1:7 and 10:29 ("his rules and his rules"), Ezra 7:10, and Numbers 27:11's "rule and rule." The phrase from "perpetual statute" now renders one way across the corpus: "a permanent rule." Leviticus formerly read "a lasting rule" at six places (3:17; 10:9; 16:29; 16:31; 16:34; 17:7) beside "a permanent rule" at others; Ruling 8 of the completion docket (2026-10-24) conformed all twenty-seven places of the family to "a permanent rule," superseding Ruling 14 of 2026-10-18 ("let both stand as they are") as to the Leviticus forms — the supersession is carried here openly, and the earlier ruling stands preserved in the record, not erased. Habakkuk, despite an early description pairing it with Leviticus in this entry, carries neither form. The Leviticus 19:37 conversion itself — "statute" to rule in the summary verse — is entered as auditor-flagged pending, following the chapter file's own placement.

## "Commandments" is not converted

The word "commandments" stands everywhere as the base gives it; it is not of the ordinance family and never becomes "rules," even inside a phrase whose other words convert — at Ephesians 2:15 "commandments" stands in the very phrase where "ordinances" becomes "rules," and the base's singular "commandment" at 6:2 stands likewise. Kindred words are kept as the different words they are: "precepts" (Greek Daniel 9:5; Colossians 2:22), "the tradition of men" (Colossians 2:8), the verb "ordained" (Galatians 3:19; Romans 13:1), "rulers" (Romans 13:3), "decrees" (Zechariah 1:6), and "oracles" (Wisdom 16:11) are all carried exactly and engage no conversion. Deuteronomy and Nehemiah both list "commandment(s)" among their kept terms.

## The kept terms: words carried exactly because no plainer word carries them

Seven terms form the lane's charter kept list, named identically across the reports: "meal offering," "trespass offering," "pleasant aroma," "make atonement for," "Tent of Meeting," "congregation," and "lest." They are kept as the base's words and flagged in the chapter apparatuses for the editor's read-through, because no plainer word was found that carries them without loss or added interpretation. Each book states each term's exact count, and absences are stated rather than passed over, so a term is claimed only in the form and at the count at which it actually occurs: "trespass offering," for example, occurs zero times in every New Testament book audited, and no instance of it is claimed anywhere it does not stand.
